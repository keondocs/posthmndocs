### Where it started

I grew up with a mother who treated food as something serious. Not in a precious way, but in a grounded one. She cooked ecological, made brown rice and buckweat, carrots and chicory, paid attention to what went into the body and lamented about the loss of fibre and nutritions in our food due to mass production.

Now I live at a distance from her, moving fast, and I forget to eat properly. Not dramatically, just quietly, the way most people around me also forget. Everyone I know is trying to live the life they want under pressure, and in that pressure, food becomes fuel at best, an afterthought at worst. And yet my mother is still there, still thinking about what her son is eating, still trying to care across the distance.

That gap between the care she offers and the speed I move at is where Fibres began.

The project is named after two kinds of fibres that seem to be losing value in our current economy: the fibres in our food, and the fibres of our community. The bonds between people. The roughage that slows digestion down. Both require time. Both are increasingly treated as optional. I keep asking myself: do they still exist? What do they look like now? Are they only visible when you actually go looking for them?

### What this is

Fibres begins with a question I keep returning to: what happens in the space between people when one of them feeds the other?

Not the food itself. Not the recipe, the culture, the aesthetics of the table. The space. The silence before someone takes a bite. The hand that stirs without thinking. The language that breaks down and the gesture that fills the gap.

I started noticing it in my own life first. Moving too fast, losing the thread back to people and places that mattered, feeling hollowed out by the speed of everything. And then noticing that the moments where that feeling lifted were almost always moments around food. A meal with my mother before I took the train back to the city. A friend reading a poem out loud while we ate in a car. My grandmother's kitchen, where language was never quite enough and somehow that was fine.

Fibres is my attempt to slow down long enough to look at those moments properly.

### What it asks

The project follows a series of people who feed and are fed. A grandson with Chilean and Surinamese roots visiting his Chilean grandmother, navigating family history through a small kitchen hatch despite not sharing a language. A dancer far from home, under so much pressure he barely eats, and what little he does consume in the margins of his day. A mother and her son walking through a forest before he leaves again. Two people sharing food in a car while one reads poetry about vulnerability out loud.

These are not stories about food. They are stories about what passes between people in the act of nourishing each other, and how much of that is never said.

What I am asking, across all of them: how do we feed each other across fractures? Across generations, languages, distances, exhaustion? What does care look like when it cannot afford to be beautiful? And what does a documentary look like when it refuses to treat food as backdrop and insists on it as something that ferments, travels, fails, comforts, organizes, and remembers?

### Where it comes from

Theoretically, the project is rooted in posthumanist thought, particularly Rosi Braidotti's concept of relational becoming. The idea that selfhood is not something we possess but something we form continuously, with and through others, human and non-human alike. Food is one of the most concrete and intimate sites where this entanglement becomes visible. Every meal is a negotiation of history, care, power, and belonging.

But the project also comes from somewhere more stubborn and less theoretical. I kept feeling like the things that actually hold people together are the things nobody makes work about. The ordinary gestures of nourishment. The politics of the kitchen. The silence between bites. The weight of feeding someone when you are also tired, also lost, also looking for something.

I wanted to make work that takes those things seriously. Not as metaphor. As matter.

### Form

The work begins as a series of intimate video portraits, shot close and slow, with attention to sound as much as image. Where it goes from there depends on what the material asks for. I am drawn to installation, to the possibility of multiple screens running asynchronously, of viewers moving through a space rather than sitting in front of it. But I am also aware that form should follow necessity, not ambition.

I am in the process of finding out what this work needs to be.
